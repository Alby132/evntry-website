<h1>How to promote your event<br/>and <em>fill the room</em></h1>

<p class="article-intro">Most events don't fail because they were a bad idea. They fail because they ran out of time to promote them. This is a step-by-step plan for independent venues — from the moment you confirm a date to the night of the show — built around what actually moves tickets, not what looks good on a marketing checklist.</p>

<div class="article-body">

## The promotion timeline

<div class="timeline-step">
  <div class="step-label">6+ weeks out</div>
  <div class="step-title">Lock the date and go live immediately</div>
  <p>The single biggest mistake venues make is waiting to "have everything ready" before announcing. The moment the date is confirmed, create the event and publish it — even if the artwork isn't finished and the lineup isn't fully confirmed. An early announcement with partial information beats a perfect announcement that goes out two weeks before the door. Early buyers are your most loyal fans; don't make them wait.</p>
</div>

<div class="timeline-step">
  <div class="step-label">4–6 weeks out</div>
  <div class="step-title">Post the anchor content</div>
  <p>This is your main promotional push — the high-effort content that earns the most reach. For a music event, this might be a short video of the artist in your venue, a reel using one of their tracks, or a well-designed poster posted to your grid. For a club night, it's a teaser mix or a behind-the-scenes post from setup. For comedy or spoken word, a short clip from a previous show works better than a graphic. <strong>Put the ticket link in your bio and reference it in every caption.</strong> Never bury the call-to-action.</p>
</div>

<div class="timeline-step">
  <div class="step-label">3 weeks out</div>
  <div class="step-title">Email your previous attendees</div>
  <p>If you've sold tickets before and collected email addresses (which you should — this is one of the main reasons to use a platform that lets you export your attendee data), now is the time to use them. A single short email to previous ticket buyers performs better than most paid ads. Keep it direct: what's happening, when, where, how to get tickets. If you can personalize it ("You came to our last jazz night — this one is going to be bigger"), even better.</p>
</div>

<div class="timeline-step">
  <div class="step-label">2 weeks out</div>
  <div class="step-title">Stories, countdowns and social proof</div>
  <p>At this point, urgency starts to matter. Use Instagram Stories to post a countdown sticker, a ticket remaining indicator (even a rough one — "only 40 tickets left" is honest if true), and any social proof you have: a screenshot of someone sharing the event, a quote from a previous attendee, or a clip that's picked up traction. Stories reach people who missed your earlier posts. Post to Stories every 2–3 days from here to the event.</p>
</div>

<div class="timeline-step">
  <div class="step-label">1 week out</div>
  <div class="step-title">Local channels and word of mouth</div>
  <p>This is the most underused stage of venue promotion. Local Facebook groups, community notice boards, neighbourhood apps, city-specific Reddit threads, local student unions — these reach people who will genuinely show up on the night and who you'd never reach with a targeted Instagram ad. Ask your artists or performers to share with their own following. Tell your bar staff to mention it to regulars. Word of mouth still beats algorithm reach for local events.</p>
</div>

<div class="timeline-step">
  <div class="step-label">48–72 hours out</div>
  <div class="step-title">The final push</div>
  <p>Post one more time to your feed and Stories with a clear "last chance" message. Share any final ticket count ("Fewer than 20 left"). Tag the artists again and ask them to reshare. Send a second short email to anyone who opened your first email but hasn't yet bought. This is also when you want to check your scanning setup, confirm door staff rotas, and make sure your ticket list is accessible offline in case of connectivity issues on the night.</p>
</div>

<div class="timeline-step">
  <div class="step-label">Day of the event</div>
  <div class="step-title">One last post, then focus on the room</div>
  <p>Post to Stories on the day — a quick clip from setup, a photo of the venue before doors open, or a simple countdown. Keep it short. Then put your phone away and focus on running the night. The best promotion for your next event is making this one great.</p>
</div>

## What actually drives ticket sales

After running through the tactics, it's worth stepping back. The channels that consistently sell the most tickets for independent venues are, in order:

1. **Email to previous attendees** — the highest conversion rate of any channel, by a wide margin
2. **Instagram Stories with ticket link in bio** — reaches your existing followers who missed your feed posts
3. **Artist or performer reshares** — borrows their audience, costs you nothing
4. **Word of mouth via staff and regulars** — underrated and free
5. **Local community groups and forums** — reaches people who won't see your social content

Paid social ads are further down the list than most venues expect. They work, but they require budget, targeting knowledge, and creative assets. For most independent venues with 50–300 capacity, filling the room through the channels above is more achievable and significantly cheaper.

## Building the list that makes next time easier

The best event promoters aren't doing heroic work every time — they're building an audience that makes each event easier to sell than the last. That means treating every event as a list-building opportunity:

- Use a ticketing platform that lets you export attendee emails
- After every event, send a short follow-up email ("Thanks for coming — here's what's next")
- Collect walk-in emails where possible with a simple sign-up (raffle, mailing list, free drink on next visit)
- Ask performers to tag your venue in their recap posts and reshare those

A venue with 500 genuine email subscribers consistently fills rooms that a venue with 5,000 Instagram followers cannot. The email list is the asset. Everything else is rented.

<div class="callout">
<p><strong>The most important rule:</strong> Start earlier than you think you need to. Every week of promotion before the event roughly doubles the chance of a sell-out. Two weeks' notice is barely enough for your most loyal regulars — it's nothing for the rest of the audience.</p>
</div>

## After the event: the step most venues skip

Send a post-event email within 48 hours. Thank attendees, share one or two photos from the night, and mention what's coming next. This single email does more for long-term ticket sales than almost any other marketing activity. It turns a one-time attendee into a repeat buyer. It keeps your venue front of mind when they're deciding where to spend their money next weekend. And it costs you ten minutes.

Most venues skip this step entirely. Don't be most venues.
